They include disorders of the heart and blood vessels, such as coronary heart disease, cerebrovascular disease, and other related conditions. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), cardiovascular diseases caused approximately 19.8 million deaths globally in 2022, representing about 32% of all deaths worldwide [1]. Advances in information and communication technology have created opportunities to improve healthcare delivery through computer-based systems. Web-based applications can support the collection, processing, storage, and analysis of patient information, enabling healthcare professionals to access relevant information and make informed decisions. In cardiovascular healthcare, machine learning techniques can be applied to patient data to identify patterns associated with cardiovascular disease and estimate the likelihood of disease occurrence [2].
One machine learning technique that can be applied to cardiovascular disease prediction is the Random Forest algorithm. Random Forest is an ensemble learning method that combines multiple decision trees to generate a prediction. It can process several patient-related variables and identify relationships between these variables and the predicted outcome [3]. Research has investigated the application of machine learning models, including Random Forest, to cardiovascular disease risk prediction using clinical and electronic health record data. However, model performance can vary depending on the dataset, selected variables, and evaluation methods used [3][4].

Such an application can combine data entry, data processing, prediction, and result presentation within a single platform. The prediction is intended to support healthcare professionals in assessing cardiovascular disease risk rather than replace clinical examination, professional judgment, or established diagnostic procedures.
This project focuses on designing and developing a web-based application to support cardiovascular disease prediction using the Random Forest algorithm at Saint Mary’s Hospital, Drobo. 2.1 Conceptual Review
2.1.1 Overview of Cardiovascular Diseases
Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) refer to a group of disorders that affect the heart and blood vessels. They include coronary heart disease, cerebrovascular disease, peripheral arterial disease, rheumatic heart disease, congenital heart disease, deep vein thrombosis, and pulmonary embolism. These conditions can disrupt the normal functioning of the cardiovascular system and can lead to serious health complications if not properly managed.
According to the World Health Organization, cardiovascular diseases are the leading cause of death globally. Approximately 19.8 million people died from cardiovascular diseases in 2022, accounting for about 32% of all deaths worldwide [1]. A substantial proportion of these deaths resulted from heart attacks and strokes. This demonstrates the importance of identifying cardiovascular disease risk factors and improving approaches to prevention, assessment, and management.
Cardiovascular diseases can develop over time through the interaction of several biological, behavioral, and environmental factors. Some conditions may remain undetected until serious symptoms occur. Consequently, identifying individuals with relevant risk factors is an important aspect of cardiovascular disease prevention. Health professionals use patient history, physical examination, laboratory investigations, and established risk assessment methods to evaluate cardiovascular health.
Cardiovascular disease is particularly important in developing countries because of changing lifestyles, urbanization, and the increasing prevalence of conditions such as hypertension, diabetes, and obesity. In Ghana, Doku et al. [2] conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of cardiovascular disease prevalence and associated risk factors. Their study included 16 studies involving 58,912 participants and estimated the pooled prevalence of cardiovascular disease in Ghana at 10.34%. The researchers also identified hypertension, diabetes, older age, and male gender among the factors associated with cardiovascular disease.
These findings indicate that cardiovascular disease is an important health concern in Ghana and support the need for approaches that can help identify relevant risk patterns. For the proposed project, cardiovascular disease provides the medical context within which the Random Forest model will process patient-related variables to generate predictions.
2.1.2 Risk Factors Associated with Cardiovascular Diseases
Risk factors are characteristics, conditions, or behaviors that increase the likelihood of developing a particular disease. Cardiovascular disease risk factors are commonly classified as modifiable or non-modifiable.
Non-modifiable risk factors
Non-modifiable risk factors are characteristics that individuals cannot easily change. They include:
Age: The likelihood of cardiovascular disease generally increases with age as changes occur in the heart and blood vessels.
Sex: Biological differences between males and females may influence cardiovascular disease patterns and risk.
Family history: A family history of cardiovascular disease may indicate an inherited susceptibility to certain cardiovascular conditions.
Genetic factors: Some inherited characteristics may influence blood pressure, cholesterol levels, and other cardiovascular risk-related conditions.

Modifiable risk factors
Modifiable risk factors are characteristics or conditions that can potentially be managed through lifestyle changes, medical intervention, or preventive measures. They include:
Hypertension: High blood pressure increases the workload of the heart and can damage blood vessels over time.
Diabetes: Elevated blood glucose levels can damage blood vessels and increase cardiovascular risk.
High cholesterol: Abnormal cholesterol levels can contribute to atherosclerosis, which involves the accumulation of fatty deposits within arteries.
Obesity: Excess body weight is associated with several cardiovascular risk factors, including hypertension, diabetes, and abnormal blood lipid levels.
Smoking: Tobacco use damages blood vessels and increases the risk of cardiovascular complications.
Physical inactivity: Insufficient physical activity may contribute to obesity, hypertension, and other conditions associated with cardiovascular disease.
Unhealthy diet: Diets containing excessive salt, saturated fats, and other unhealthy components may contribute to cardiovascular risk.
The importance of these risk factors has also been demonstrated in Ghanaian research. Li et al. [13] conducted a national study involving 1,106 Ghanaian adults and examined cardiometabolic risk factors and early signs of organ damage. Their findings reported age-standardized prevalence estimates of 26.1% for hypertension, 15.1% for obesity, and 6.8% for diabetes among the study participants. The study further identified relationships between certain risk factors and indicators of organ damage.
Similarly, Doku et al. [2] identified hypertension and diabetes among factors associated with cardiovascular disease in Ghana. These findings demonstrate the relevance of considering patient-related clinical and demographic information when developing cardiovascular disease prediction systems.
For the proposed application, the variables used as model inputs will depend on the selected dataset and the availability of reliable patient information. Variables such as age, blood pressure, cholesterol, glucose-related measurements, and other relevant characteristics may be included where the dataset supports them. The application should not assume that every possible risk factor is available or required by the trained model.
2.1.3 Cardiovascular Disease Prediction
Cardiovascular disease prediction involves using available patient information to estimate the likelihood or classification of a cardiovascular disease-related outcome. Prediction systems analyze selected variables and apply mathematical or computational methods to identify patterns associated with the target outcome.
Traditional cardiovascular risk assessment methods commonly use established statistical models and clinical risk charts. These approaches combine selected risk factors to estimate an individual's probability of experiencing a cardiovascular event within a specified period. The World Health Organization CVD Risk Chart Working Group developed revised cardiovascular risk charts for 21 global regions to support cardiovascular risk estimation [14].
Although traditional risk charts are important, machine learning provides additional methods for analyzing complex relationships among several variables. Machine learning models can identify patterns within datasets and use these patterns to generate predictions for new observations.
It is important to distinguish between cardiovascular disease classification and long-term cardiovascular risk estimation. Classification generally predicts whether a patient belongs to a particular outcome category, such as having or not having cardiovascular disease, based on the labels used in the training dataset. Long-term risk estimation predicts the probability of experiencing a future cardiovascular event over a defined period.
The nature of the prediction depends on the dataset, the outcome variable, and the model design. Therefore, the proposed application will perform the prediction task supported by its selected dataset rather than automatically claim to estimate future cardiovascular risk.
In the context of this project, cardiovascular disease prediction refers to using the trained Random Forest model to analyze relevant patient information and produce a prediction through a web-based application. The prediction will be presented as decision-support information for authorized healthcare professionals.
2.1.4 Machine Learning in Healthcare
Machine learning is a branch of artificial intelligence that enables computer systems to learn patterns from data and use those patterns to perform tasks such as classification, prediction, and decision support. Unlike systems that depend entirely on manually written rules, machine learning models identify relationships within training data and use the learned relationships when processing new information.
Machine learning has attracted attention in healthcare because medical data often contain several variables that may be related to a particular health condition. These variables may include demographic characteristics, laboratory results, clinical measurements, medical history, and other patient information. Machine learning techniques can analyze such data to identify patterns that may not be easily recognized through simple manual analysis.
Azmi et al. [3] reviewed machine learning approaches used for cardiovascular disease prediction with medical big data. Their review discussed the application of different machine learning techniques to cardiovascular disease prediction and highlighted the growing role of computational approaches in analyzing medical information.
Machine learning applications in healthcare can be grouped into different categories based on the task being performed:
Classification: Classification models assign input data to predefined categories. In cardiovascular disease prediction, a classification model may categorize patient information into outcome classes defined by the training dataset.
Regression: Regression models estimate continuous numerical values. In healthcare, these models may estimate measurements or numerical risk values.
Clustering: Clustering methods group similar data observations without requiring predefined outcome categories. They may be used to identify patterns or groups within patient datasets.
Anomaly detection: These methods identify observations that differ significantly from the general pattern of the data. They may be useful for identifying unusual measurements or data-quality problems.
For this project, classification is the relevant machine learning task if the selected dataset contains predefined cardiovascular disease outcome categories. The Random Forest algorithm will be trained to learn relationships between the input variables and the outcome labels.
However, machine learning predictions are dependent on the quality, representativeness, and suitability of the training data. 2.1.5 Types of Machine Learning Algorithms Used in Healthcare
Several machine learning algorithms have been applied to healthcare prediction problems. The choice of an algorithm depends on the nature of the data, the prediction task, computational requirements, and the desired performance.
Logistic Regression
Logistic Regression is a statistical learning technique commonly used for binary classification. It estimates the probability that an observation belongs to a particular class based on a combination of input variables. In cardiovascular disease prediction, it may be used to estimate the probability of a patient belonging to a disease or non-disease category.
Logistic Regression is relatively easy to interpret and can show the relationship between predictor variables and the outcome. However, its standard form may not adequately represent complex non-linear relationships unless suitable transformations and interaction terms are included.
Decision Tree
A Decision Tree is a supervised learning algorithm that represents decisions through a tree-like structure. It consists of a root node, internal decision nodes, branches, and leaf nodes. The algorithm repeatedly divides the dataset based on selected features until a prediction or classification is reached.
Decision Trees are useful because their decision-making process can be represented in an understandable structure. However, a single decision tree may become overly complex and overfit the training data if it is not properly controlled.
Support Vector Machine
Support Vector Machine (SVM) is a supervised learning algorithm used for classification and regression. It aims to identify a decision boundary that separates observations into different classes. SVM can also use kernel functions to represent non-linear relationships in data.
In healthcare prediction, SVM can classify patients based on clinical variables. Its performance, however, may depend on the selection of kernel functions and parameter settings.
K-Nearest Neighbors
K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) is a classification technique that predicts the class of a new observation based on the classes of nearby observations in the training dataset. It calculates distances between observations and uses the nearest neighbors to determine the predicted class.
KNN is relatively straightforward to implement, but its performance can be affected by the scale of input variables, the number of neighbors selected, and irrelevant features.
Artificial Neural Networks
Artificial Neural Networks are computational models inspired by the structure of biological neural networks. They consist of interconnected processing units that learn patterns through training. Neural networks can represent complex relationships in data and have been applied to different healthcare prediction tasks.
However, neural networks may require larger datasets and more computational resources, and their internal decision-making processes can be difficult to interpret.

Random Forest
Random Forest is an ensemble learning algorithm that combines multiple decision trees to produce a final prediction. It uses random sampling of observations and randomly selects features during tree construction. For classification, the final output is commonly determined through majority voting among the individual trees.
Random Forest is relevant to this project because it can analyze several input variables and capture complex relationships within structured datasets. Its operation and advantages are discussed in greater detail in the following sections.
2.1.6 Random Forest Algorithm
Random Forest is a supervised machine learning algorithm used for classification and regression tasks. Leo Breiman introduced it in 2001 as an ensemble method that combines multiple decision tree predictors [5].
The algorithm is based on the principle that a collection of decision trees can produce more stable predictions than a single tree. Each tree is constructed using a random sample of the training data, while a random subset of features is considered when determining splits. The predictions from the individual trees are then combined to produce the final output.
For classification tasks, the Random Forest algorithm commonly uses majority voting. Each decision tree predicts a class, and the class with the most votes becomes the final prediction.
For example, if a forest contains 100 decision trees and 72 trees predict one class while 28 predict another, the class receiving 72 votes becomes the final classification. This example illustrates the voting process; it does not represent the expected performance of the proposed project.

How Random Forest works
The general operation of Random Forest involves the following steps:
Training data preparation: A labeled dataset is provided for model training. The dataset contains input variables and corresponding outcome labels.
Bootstrap sampling: Random samples are drawn from the training dataset, usually with replacement, to create training subsets for individual trees.
Decision tree construction: A decision tree is trained using each sampled subset. At each split, a randomly selected subset of features is considered.
Tree generation: The process is repeated to construct multiple decision trees, forming a forest.
Prediction: When new patient data are supplied, each tree independently generates a prediction.
Majority voting: The predictions from all trees are combined, and the most frequently predicted class is selected as the final classification.
Advantages of Random Forest
Random Forest offers several advantages that make it suitable for investigating structured healthcare prediction problems:
It combines several decision trees rather than depending on one tree.
It can model non-linear relationships between input variables and the target outcome.
It can work with datasets containing several predictor variables.
It provides feature-importance measures that can help identify variables contributing to model predictions.
It is generally more resistant to overfitting than a single unrestricted decision tree, although overfitting and other forms of model error are still possible.
It can be applied to both classification and regression tasks.
Breiman [5] explained that Random Forest performance depends partly on the strength of individual trees and the correlation among them. Random feature selection helps reduce correlation between trees and contributes to the ensemble's performance.
Limitations of Random Forest
Despite its advantages, Random Forest also has limitations:
A large number of trees may increase computational and memory requirements.
The combined decision process may be more difficult to interpret than a single decision tree.
Model performance depends on the quality and representativeness of the training dataset.
Class imbalance may affect predictions, particularly where one outcome category is much more common than another.
Feature-importance measures do not automatically establish causal relationships between variables and cardiovascular disease.
A model that performs well on training data may not necessarily perform well on new patient data.
These limitations make it necessary to evaluate the trained model using data that were not used during training and to examine its performance using appropriate metrics.

2.1.9 Integration of Machine Learning Models into Web Applications
Integrating a machine learning model into a web application involves connecting the trained model to the software environment through which users submit data and receive predictions. This allows users to access the model's functionality without directly interacting with the underlying programming code.
The integration process generally involves the following stages:
Model development: The Random Forest algorithm is trained using a suitable dataset.
Model evaluation: The trained model is evaluated using appropriate performance metrics and test data.
Model saving: The trained model and required preprocessing components are saved in a format that the application can load.
Backend integration: The application backend loads the model and provides a function or service for processing prediction requests.
Input submission: The user enters the required patient information through the web interface.
Data validation and preprocessing: The application checks the input values and applies the required transformations.
Prediction generation: The processed values are passed to the Random Forest model.
Result presentation: The application displays the prediction to the authorized user.

Failure to maintain this consistency may lead to incorrect predictions.

2.1.10 Evaluation of Machine Learning Prediction Models
Evaluation is an important stage in machine learning because it determines how well a trained model performs on data that it has not previously encountered. A model should not be considered reliable merely because it produces high accuracy on its training dataset.
Prediction models are commonly evaluated using a separate test dataset or appropriate validation procedures. This helps estimate how the model may perform on new observations.
The following metrics are relevant to classification-based cardiovascular disease prediction:
Accuracy
Accuracy measures the proportion of all correct predictions.
Accuracy=(TP+TN)/(TP+TN+FP+FN)

Where:
TP = True Positives
TN = True Negatives
FP = False Positives
FN = False Negatives
Precision
Precision measures the proportion of positive predictions that are actually positive.
Precision=TP/(TP+FP)
Recall (Sensitivity)
Recall measures the proportion of actual positive cases correctly identified by the model.
Recall=TP/(TP+FN)
Specificity
Specificity measures the proportion of actual negative cases correctly identified by the model.
Specificity=TN/(TN+FP)
F1-Score
The F1-score is the harmonic mean of precision and recall.
F1"-" Score=2×(Precision×Recall)/(Precision+Recall)

Confusion Matrix
A confusion matrix presents the number of correct and incorrect predictions made by a classification model. It contains true positives, true negatives, false positives, and false negatives. It helps identify the types of errors the model makes.
Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (ROC-AUC)
The ROC curve illustrates the relationship between sensitivity and the false-positive rate at different classification thresholds. The area under the curve measures how well the model distinguishes between the outcome classes.
The choice of evaluation metrics is important in healthcare because different types of prediction errors may have different consequences. For example, a false negative occurs when the model predicts that a patient does not belong to the positive class even though the actual outcome is positive. In a cardiovascular disease prediction application, such an error may be important because it could result in a potentially relevant case not being identified by the model.
Collins et al. [16] explain that prediction models require meaningful evaluation to establish their performance in the populations and settings in which they are intended to be used. They emphasize the importance of assessing discrimination, calibration, and generalizability. Therefore, the proposed model will be evaluated using appropriate classification metrics, and its performance will be interpreted in relation to the selected dataset and prediction task.
2.1.11 Data Preprocessing in Machine Learning
Data preprocessing refers to preparing raw data before it is supplied to a machine learning algorithm. Healthcare datasets may contain missing values, inconsistent entries, duplicate records, different measurement scales, and categorical variables that require transformation.
The quality of data preprocessing can influence a prediction model's performance. Inappropriate handling of missing values, incorrect data types, or inconsistent variable transformations may affect the model's ability to learn meaningful patterns.
Common data preprocessing activities include:
Data cleaning: Identifying and correcting errors, inconsistent values, and duplicate records.
Handling missing values: Selecting an appropriate method for dealing with incomplete observations.
Encoding categorical variables: Converting non-numerical variables into numerical representations where required by the model.
Feature selection: Identifying variables relevant to the prediction task.
Data splitting: Dividing the dataset into training and testing subsets to support model development and evaluation.
Feature scaling: Adjusting the numerical scale of variables where necessary for the selected processing pipeline.
Random Forest generally does not require feature scaling in the same way that distance-based algorithms such as KNN do. However, data validation, correct variable encoding, and consistent handling of missing values remain important.
The proposed project will prepare the selected dataset before model training. The preprocessing procedures used during training will also be applied consistently when new patient information is submitted through the web application.
2.1.12 Data Security and Privacy in Healthcare Applications
Healthcare applications may process sensitive personal information, including patient names, medical histories, laboratory results, and other health-related information. Such information requires appropriate protection to prevent unauthorized access, misuse, alteration, or disclosure.
Data security refers to the technical and administrative measures used to protect information and systems. Data privacy concerns the appropriate collection, use, storage, and sharing of personal information.
Important security mechanisms in web-based healthcare applications include:
Authentication: Authentication verifies the identity of users before allowing access to the application. It may involve usernames, passwords, or other verification mechanisms.
Authorization: Authorization determines what an authenticated user is permitted to access or perform within the system. For example, different user roles may have different permissions.
Password protection: Store passwords using secure password-hashing methods rather than in plain text.
Secure communication: Appropriate secure communication protocols should be used to protect information transmitted between the user's browser and the application server.
Input validation: Validate user-submitted information to reduce errors and prevent malicious or unexpected input.
Access control: Restrict access to patient-related information to users with appropriate permissions.

2.2 Theoretical Review
The theoretical review discusses the principles that explain how the proposed prediction application operates. The project is based primarily on supervised machine learning, ensemble learning, and decision tree theory. These concepts provide the foundation for understanding how the Random Forest algorithm learns from labeled data and generates predictions.
2.2.1 Supervised Learning Theory
Supervised learning is a machine learning approach in which an algorithm learns from a dataset containing input variables and known output labels. During training, the algorithm examines the relationship between the input variables and their corresponding outputs. It then uses the learned relationship to predict the output for new observations.
A supervised learning dataset can be represented as:
D={(x*1,y_1 ),(x_2,y_2 ),…,(x_n,y_n )}
Where:
D = The training dataset.
x_i = The input variables for observation i.
y_i = The known outcome associated with observation i.
n = The total number of observations.
In the proposed project, the input variables will consist of the patient characteristics available in the selected cardiovascular disease dataset. The output variable will represent the cardiovascular disease outcome defined by that dataset.
During training, the Random Forest algorithm learns patterns connecting the input variables to the outcome labels. Once training is completed, the model can process new patient information and generate a prediction.
Supervised learning is appropriate for the proposed project because the intended prediction task requires a dataset containing known cardiovascular disease outcomes. The model can therefore learn from examples in which the relationship between patient information and the target outcome is already established.
2.2.2 Decision Tree Theory
Decision Tree theory provides one of the main foundations of the Random Forest algorithm. A decision tree is a supervised learning model that divides a dataset into smaller groups based on selected features. Decision nodes and branches represent the divisions, while leaf nodes represent the final predictions.
Constructing a decision tree involves selecting features that effectively separate observations into different outcome categories. Common criteria used for selecting splits include Gini impurity and information gain based on entropy.
Gini impurity can be expressed as:
Gini Impurity:
Gini=1-∑*(i=1)^k▒p_i^2
Where:
k = Number of classes.
pi = Proportion of observations belonging to class i.
A lower Gini impurity indicates that a node contains observations that are more concentrated in particular classes. Decision trees use such measures to identify useful divisions within the training data.
In cardiovascular disease prediction, a decision tree may split patient observations based on variables such as age, blood pressure, cholesterol, or other available clinical measurements. The resulting branches eventually lead to an outcome category.
Although decision trees are relatively easy to interpret, individual trees can be sensitive to changes in training data. Random Forest addresses some of these limitations by constructing and combining multiple trees instead of relying on a single tree.
2.2.3 Ensemble Learning Theory
Ensemble learning is a machine learning approach in which multiple models are combined to produce a final prediction. The central idea is that combining several models can produce more stable or accurate results than relying on a single model, provided that the individual models contribute useful and sufficiently diverse predictions.
Ensemble methods include bagging, boosting, and stacking.
Bagging (Bootstrap Aggregating): Bagging involves generating multiple training samples from the original dataset, training separate models on those samples, and combining their predictions. This approach can reduce the variability of individual models.
Boosting: Boosting builds models sequentially, with later models attempting to improve on errors made by earlier models.
Stacking: Stacking combines predictions from different models using another model, commonly referred to as a meta-learner.
Random Forest is an ensemble learning method that uses bootstrap sampling and randomized feature selection to construct multiple decision trees [5]. The individual tree predictions are combined to produce the final classification.
Ensemble learning is relevant to the proposed project because it provides the theoretical basis for using multiple decision trees to predict cardiovascular disease. Instead of relying on a single tree's decision, the Random Forest model combines the outputs of several trees.
2.2.4 Bootstrap Aggregating and Random Feature Selection
Bootstrap aggregating, commonly known as bagging, is an important process used in Random Forest. It involves creating multiple training samples by randomly selecting observations from the original dataset with replacement.
Because sampling is performed with replacement, an observation may appear more than once in a particular sample, while some observations may not appear in that sample. Each sample is used to train an individual decision tree.
Random feature selection is another important characteristic of Random Forest. Instead of considering all available features at every decision-tree split, the algorithm considers a randomly selected subset of features. This introduces diversity among the trees and helps reduce their correlation.
Breiman [5] explained that Random Forest performance is influenced by the strength of individual trees and the correlation between them. The combination of bootstrap sampling and random feature selection contributes to the ensemble's ability to produce predictions.
In the proposed application, these principles will be applied during the training of the Random Forest model using the selected cardiovascular disease dataset.
2.2.5 Application of the Theories to the Proposed System
The theories discussed above provide the foundation for developing the proposed cardiovascular disease prediction application.
Supervised learning explains how the model learns from labeled patient data. Decision Tree theory explains how individual trees make predictions based on selected input variables. Ensemble learning explains why multiple trees are combined, while bootstrap aggregating and random feature selection explain how the Random Forest algorithm introduces variation among the trees.
The relationship between these theories and the proposed application can be summarized as follows:
Theory Application in the project
Supervised learning Training the model using patient input variables and known cardiovascular disease outcomes
Decision Tree theory Constructing individual trees that classify patient records
Ensemble learning Combining predictions from multiple decision trees
Bootstrap aggregating Generating training samples for individual trees
Random feature selection Introducing diversity among decision trees

Together, these theories explain how the proposed system will learn from patient data and generate cardiovascular disease predictions.

2.3.1 Studies on Cardiovascular Disease and Risk Factors in Ghana
Doku et al. [2] conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis to examine the prevalence of cardiovascular disease and associated risk factors in Ghana. The researchers searched several academic databases, including PubMed, Google Scholar, the Cochrane Library, ScienceDirect, and African Journals Online. Sixteen studies involving 58,912 participants were included in the review.
The findings indicated a pooled cardiovascular disease prevalence of 10.34% in Ghana. The study also identified hypertension, diabetes, older age, and male gender as factors associated with cardiovascular disease. The authors concluded that cardiovascular disease represents an important public health concern in Ghana and that interventions are needed to address the disease burden and its associated risk factors.
The study is relevant to the proposed project because it provides Ghana-specific evidence on cardiovascular disease and supports the need to investigate technological approaches that can facilitate disease prediction. However, the study focused on estimating disease prevalence and examining associated risk factors rather than developing a web-based Random Forest prediction application.
Li et al. [13] investigated cardiometabolic risk factors and preclinical target organ damage among adults in Ghana. The study involved 1,106 participants selected through a stratified multistage sampling approach. The researchers examined risk factors including hypertension, obesity, diabetes, and hyperuricemia, as well as indicators of early organ damage.
The study reported that hypertension, obesity, and diabetes were present among the Ghanaian adult population examined. It also identified relationships between some cardiometabolic risk factors and indicators of organ damage. The findings provide evidence of the relevance of clinical and demographic variables in understanding cardiovascular health in Ghana.
This study is important to the proposed project because it demonstrates the availability of Ghanaian research on cardiovascular risk factors. However, it did not investigate integrating a machine learning model into a web-based application for cardiovascular disease prediction.

This study is relevant to the proposed project because it examines machine learning methods for cardiovascular disease prediction. Liu et al. [4] conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of machine learning-based cardiovascular disease risk prediction models developed using electronic health record data. The researchers identified 32 machine learning models and 26 conventional statistical models across 20 selected studies. Their findings indicated that Random Forest and deep learning approaches demonstrated promising predictive performance. However, the review also reported substantial heterogeneity between studies and potential publication bias. The authors emphasized the need for improved methodological transparency and standardization to strengthen the reliability and clinical usefulness of machine learning-based cardiovascular disease prediction.
This study is particularly relevant because it examines machine learning models using electronic health record data and discusses the performance of Random Forest in cardiovascular disease risk prediction.
